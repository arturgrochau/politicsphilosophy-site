The vocabulary of decline

Eighteen terms the tradition actually uses, with who coined them and what they were arguing against. Most get thrown around loosely. These are the definitions their authors would recognise.

anakyklosis Greek
The cycle of constitutions. Kingship decays into tyranny, aristocracy into oligarchy, democracy into mob rule, and then a strongman restores kingship and the wheel turns again. Polybius thought the engine was generational: heirs raised inside inherited power lose whatever won it.
Polybius, Histories VI
asabiyya Arabic
Group feeling. The solidarity produced by kinship and shared hardship, strongest among tribal peoples and weakest in comfortable cities. Ibn Khaldun made it the thing political power actually rests on, and gave dynasties roughly four generations before it dissolves in luxury.
Ibn Khaldun, Muqaddimah, 1377
stasis Greek
Faction, or civil strife. Aristotle's diagnosis is that every party to it appeals to equality and each grasps only half: democrats reason from equal birth to equality in everything, oligarchs from unequal wealth to inequality in everything.
Aristotle, Politics V
politeia symmikte Greek
The mixed constitution. Combining monarchic, aristocratic and democratic elements so each checks the others, which Polybius thought was how Rome escaped the cycle for as long as it did. The ancestor of separation of powers.
Polybius; later Cicero and Machiavelli
ochlocracy Greek
Mob rule. The degenerate form of democracy in the Polybian scheme, arriving when the people have been corrupted by bribery and the appetite for office.
Polybius
virtu Italian
The capacity of a people or a founder to act effectively in politics, closer to force of character than to moral virtue. Its opposite is corruzione, the state in which citizens can no longer be trusted with free institutions.
Machiavelli, Discourses on Livy, c. 1517
metus hostilis Latin
Fear of the enemy. Sallust's claim is that while Carthage stood, dread of a rival held Romans to discipline and concord, and that the ruin began in 146 BC when the rival was destroyed. An external threat as a source of internal cohesion.
Sallust, Bellum Catilinae, c. 42 BC
corsi e ricorsi Italian
Courses and recourses. Vico's cycle of three ages, of gods, of heroes and of men, ending in the barbarie della riflessione, the barbarism of reflection, which he thought worse than the original barbarism because it is cold rather than savage.
Vico, The New Science, 1725
anomie French
Not moral decay but deregulation. Human desire has no natural limit, so it needs external norms to bound it. When rapid change outruns those norms, including sudden prosperity, the result is the restlessness Durkheim measured against suicide rates.
Durkheim, Suicide, 1897
Entzauberung German
Disenchantment of the world. Weber's point is not that we now know more, but that we believe in principle that everything can be mastered by calculation, so no mysterious incalculable forces are left in play.
Weber, lecture 'Science as a Vocation', 1917
stahlhartes Gehause German
A shell as hard as steel. Rendered by Talcott Parsons in 1930 as the 'iron cage', which is the phrase everyone now quotes. Weber's own image is a housing we are born into rather than a prison we are locked in, which changes the argument.
Weber, The Protestant Ethic, 1905
decadence French
A physiological and spiritual exhaustion in which the instincts turn against life, and weakness gets revalued as virtue. Nietzsche's version of decline is a diagnosis of values, not of institutions.
Nietzsche, Twilight of the Idols, 1889
Kultur / Zivilisation German
Spengler's distinction between the creative, growing phase of a high culture and its rigid, urban, imperial late phase. Treat it as a description of a mood rather than a finding: specialists identified serious factual errors early, and the work was later taken up by the radical right.
Spengler, The Decline of the West, 1918
challenge and response English
Toynbee's alternative to Spengler's biological determinism. A challenge of the right severity, neither trivial nor crushing, draws a solution out of a creative minority, which the majority copies. Growth stops when that minority stops creating and starts merely dominating.
Toynbee, A Study of History, 1934
hombre-masa Spanish
Mass-man. Not a class but a psychological type found in every stratum, including the professions: the person who finds himself like everybody else and is pleased about it, who demands the benefits of a civilisation without asking what maintains it.
Ortega y Gasset, The Revolt of the Masses, 1930
declining marginal returns English
Societies solve problems by adding complexity, and each added layer costs more and returns less. When the return on further complexity goes negative, collapse becomes the economic option rather than the catastrophe. Tainter defines collapse narrowly, as a rapid loss of complexity.
Tainter, The Collapse of Complex Societies, 1988
elite overproduction English
Too many credentialed people chasing too few elite positions. In Turchin's structural-demographic model it combines with popular immiseration and state fiscal stress to produce the disintegrative phase of a cycle.
Turchin, Secular Cycles, 2009
liquid modernity English
Bauman's claim that modernity melted the old solids and then stopped casting new ones. Power becomes light and mobile while politics stays local, so institutions can no longer hold the shape of a life.
Bauman, Liquid Modernity, 2000
